# Plain-English Analysis of OCC Bulletin 2026-13

Source: https://www.occ.treas.gov/news-issuances/bulletins/2026/bulletin-2026-13.html

---

# What This Document Is

The following U.S. banking regulators:

- Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC)
- Federal Reserve System
- Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC)

are updating guidance for how banks should manage **risk from mathematical models and algorithms**.

---

# In Simple Terms

Banks use models for many critical business functions, including:

- deciding whether to approve loans
- detecting fraud
- pricing financial products
- estimating losses
- anti-money laundering detection
- forecasting risk
- stress testing

The regulators are essentially saying:

> Banks are increasingly dependent on models and algorithms. Those systems can fail, drift, hallucinate, or produce harmful outcomes. Banks need processes to control and monitor those risks.

---

# The Big Shift

## Old Approach (2011)

The older guidance was often interpreted as:

- rigid
- documentation-heavy
- highly procedural
- compliance-centric

Banks frequently treated the guidance as:

> “Validate every model constantly and produce enormous amounts of paperwork.”

---

## New Approach (2026)

The updated guidance introduces a more pragmatic philosophy:

> “Use common sense and proportionality.”

Meaning:

- large and complex models require stronger oversight
- small and simple systems require lighter controls

This is commonly referred to as a:

> **Risk-based approach**

---

# Important Clarifications

# 1. Not Everything Is Considered a “Model”

The OCC now clarifies that simple systems are NOT necessarily “models.”

## These are generally NOT considered models:

- spreadsheets
- arithmetic formulas
- deterministic rules
- straightforward software logic

## These ARE considered models:

- statistical systems
- machine learning systems
- probabilistic forecasting
- credit risk algorithms

## Why this matters

This significantly lowers the compliance burden for many traditional software systems and operational tools.

---

# 2. Generative AI Is NOT Covered Yet

This is one of the most important aspects of the bulletin.

The regulators explicitly state that:

- ChatGPT-style systems
- agentic AI
- generative AI

are:

> “novel and rapidly evolving”

…and are NOT fully covered by this guidance yet.

## Translation

The regulators are acknowledging:

- AI governance standards are immature
- the technology is evolving rapidly
- they are not ready to issue comprehensive AI governance rules yet

In effect:

> The regulators are buying time while they determine how AI should ultimately be governed safely.

---

# 3. The Guidance Primarily Targets Large Banks

The guidance is mainly aimed at:

- banks with more than $30 billion in assets

Smaller institutions may:

- apply simplified controls
- adopt lighter governance
- only require deeper oversight if using sophisticated modeling systems

---

# 4. Regulators Want Governance More Than Mathematical Perfection

The regulators care less about:

- the exact mathematical technique used

…and more about:

- oversight
- validation
- accountability
- documentation
- monitoring
- escalation procedures

In plain English:

> Someone inside the organization must understand the model well enough to recognize when it is wrong or behaving dangerously.

---

# 5. Third-Party Vendors Are Included

This is extremely important for fintech and SaaS vendors.

Banks cannot simply say:

> “The vendor built it, so it’s the vendor’s responsibility.”

If a bank uses:

- third-party fraud software
- underwriting systems
- AI tooling
- SaaS analytics platforms

…the bank still remains responsible for understanding and managing the associated risks.

## Major implications for:

- fintech companies
- SaaS providers
- AI risk platforms
- RegTech startups
- compliance tooling vendors

---

# Why This Matters Technically

This bulletin quietly signals a major transition in the financial industry.

The regulators are acknowledging the reality of:

- modern AI/ML complexity
- model sprawl
- cloud SaaS dependency
- opaque vendor systems
- probabilistic decision-making systems

At the same time, they are also effectively saying:

> “We do not yet fully understand how generative AI should be regulated.”

That is a very significant admission.

---

# The Most Important Practical Sentence

One of the most important lines in the document is:

> “This guidance does not set forth enforceable standards or prescriptive requirements.”

## Meaning

- this is guidance, not statutory law
- examiners will use it directionally
- institutions are expected to behave reasonably
- there is flexibility in implementation

# Core Message in One Sentence

The regulators are effectively saying:

> Banks may use advanced models and AI systems, but they must understand, monitor, validate, and govern them proportionally to the risk those systems create.

---

# Reading Between the Lines

The broader subtext appears to be:

- regulators know AI adoption is accelerating rapidly
- they do not want to crush innovation
- they also want to avoid another systemic financial crisis caused by opaque algorithms
- they are preparing the banking industry for future AI governance frameworks

This bulletin appears to be an early transitional step toward more formal AI governance regulation in the financial sector.
